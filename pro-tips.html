Pro Tips

Advanced techniques for players who already know how to flip

📐 Thinking in Momentum, Not Buttons

The Arc Equation

Every jump in Flipping is Hard is a projectile with a rotating body attached. That means two separate problems have to be solved at once: where Flippy goes, and how he is oriented when he arrives. Beginners solve them sequentially — land, then think about the next jump. Experts solve them in parallel.

  • • Horizontal speed comes from run momentum and shake charge, and it is only spent on travel.
  • • Vertical speed comes from the flip's launch impulse, and it is spent on height and hang time.
  • • Rotation is a separate budget: a full flip is one "beat" of time you have spent solving the first two.

Spending the Right Budget

The classic mistake is over-spending rotation. If you flip three times across a gap that only needs one, you land facing sideways or upside down and slide off. Under-spending is safer: an extra half rotation can be corrected in the air, but a full extra flip cannot.

  • • Wide gap, low wall — spend rotation on speed, keep the flip count low.
  • • Short gap, high wall — flip more, because the wall absorbs the overshoot.
  • • Narrow ledge — arrive flat. Never arrive mid-rotation.

Rotation Reference

Jump type Best used for Rotation spend Key risk
Single flip hop Short gaps, platform edges Minimal Not enough distance if you charge fully
Double flip Standard mid-length gaps Medium Late correction leaves no margin
Triple flip Long chasms, high arcs Heavy Arriving rotated if the arc is short
Shake-charged leap Vertical climbs, wall transfers Choose deliberately Extra height without extra reach

🎥 Camera and Line-of-Sight Framing

Look Ahead, Not Down

When you jump, the camera keeps Flippy roughly centred, so what you lose is the ground you just left. Look at the landing platform before you commit, not during the arc — mid-air camera movement is how people walk into the backs of walls.

Read the Silhouette

Platforms are easier to judge as shapes than as surfaces. Before a long jump, note how many body lengths of empty space separate the two ledges. Your own height is a free ruler — use Flippy himself as the measuring stick.

Bank the Angles

Diagonal jumps are easier to reason about than straight ones because you can see both the take-off and the landing at once. Set up diagonal lines wherever the level geometry allows, and reserve straight jumps for corridors.

👥 Coordinating a Full Lobby

Split the Roles

With 16 phones in a lobby, nobody can watch everything. Assign an anchor who owns the route, spotter players who watch moving platforms, and freelancers who test risky lines and report back. Without roles, a large group just fails loudly at the same time.

Call Landmarks, Not Directions

"Left" and "right" get confused instantly across a team. Call stable world features instead — the tall pillar, the spinning bar, the red door. If your callout would mean different things to two players, it is not a callout yet.

Reset in Small Units

When a group fails repeatedly, shrink the unit. One player clears the hard section alone to learn it, then the rest follow the same line. Teams that attempt a new section with sixteen phones usually end up with sixteen slightly different bad routes.

🏋️ Training Drills That Actually Build Skill

  • Repeat drill: Pick the first gap of a chapter and attempt it ten times in a row without progressing. Consistency on a single obstacle is what actually clears the later ones.
  • Rotation drill: Deliberately land on the far edge of a wide platform instead of the middle. It builds the confidence to correct late and keeps you honest about where your arc actually ends.
  • Momentum drill: Run a full stretch of a level and stop moving dead. The distance where you lose speed shows you exactly which transitions need work.
  • Charge drill: Time the shake release by sound and feel rather than by watching the meter, so your hands learn the rhythm while your eyes stay on the route.
  • Blind call drill: In co-op, have one player describe the next platform purely by shape while the other jumps. It exposes how much your team relies on shared sight lines.

🧩 Failure Patterns and Their Fixes

Short landing, fast rotation

You clear the gap but land rotated. Fix: drop one flip from the pattern and let the extra speed be spent on the run-up instead.

Landed but sliding off

Contact happened at the rim. Fix: aim for the middle of the platform and accept a shorter jump rather than a longer one.

Running out of momentum

You stop between jumps and rebuild from zero. Fix: land and re-launch immediately, even with a smaller flip, to keep the chain alive.

Overcharged and uncontrollable

The shake auto-released at maximum while you were still turning. Fix: start charging earlier and release on your own count.
